What is a terrarium?

A terrarium is a transparent glass or plastic container that houses soil and live plants, creating a self-sustaining or semi-managed indoor ecosystem.

Inside a closed jar, the same water gets used over and over. Plants release it through their leaves, it fogs up the glass, and it trickles back into the soil: a small version of the water cycle outside. Light keeps the plants growing, and tiny decomposers such as springtails, fungi and bacteria break dead leaves down into nutrients the plants can use again.

Closed terrarium

Lid on. Water recycles inside, so it may need watering only every few months. Suits humidity lovers like ferns, nerve plants and moss.

Open terrarium

No lid. Moisture escapes into the room, so you water more often. Suits plants that like dry air, like succulents and cacti.

    Build a real terrarium from scratch

    Each step matches a part of the builder above. Read what to do and why it matters, try it on screen, then build it in glass.

    What you’ll need

    • A clear glass or plastic container (with a lid for a closed terrarium)
    • Gravel or clay pebbles (LECA) for drainage
    • Fine mesh or a handful of sphagnum moss
    • Activated charcoal (optional)
    • Potting soil, or cactus mix for succulents
    • Two to five small plants with the same needs
    • Stones, cleaned wood or moss for decoration
    • A spoon, chopsticks or long tweezers, a spray bottle, a small brush and paper towels

    Wash your hands after handling soil, and wear gloves when planting cacti.

    1. 1

      Choose and clean a container

      Pick a clear container with an opening wide enough for your hand or tools. Wash it with soap and warm water and let it dry completely. Decide now whether it will be closed (with a lid) or open.

      Why it matters: Clear walls let light reach the plants. Leftover dirt or residue can feed mold and algae, and the lid decision controls how much moisture stays inside.

    2. 2

      Plan plants that share a climate

      Choose small, slow-growing plants that like the same conditions. For a closed jar, try nerve plants (fittonia), small ferns, pilea and moss. For an open container, try succulents and cacti. Don’t mix the two groups.

      Why it matters: One container has one climate. A fern needs damp, humid air, while a cactus rots in it. Small plants won’t outgrow the glass in a few weeks.

    3. 3

      Add a drainage layer

      Pour in 2 to 5 cm (about 1 to 2 inches) of gravel or clay pebbles across the bottom.

      Why it matters: A terrarium has no drainage hole. Extra water collects down here instead of soaking the roots, which prevents root rot.

    4. 4

      Add a barrier, and charcoal if you like

      Lay a piece of fine mesh or a thin layer of sphagnum moss over the drainage. If you’re using activated charcoal, sprinkle about 1 cm (half an inch) on top.

      Why it matters: The barrier stops soil from sifting down and clogging the drainage. Many builders add charcoal to help with odors; it’s optional, and it won’t make up for overwatering.

    5. 5

      Add soil and shape the ground

      Add 5 to 8 cm (2 to 3 inches) of soil, deeper than your plants’ roots. Use potting soil for a closed jar or cactus mix for succulents. Slope it higher toward the back or build a small hill.

      Why it matters: Soil anchors the roots and holds water and nutrients. A slope or hill makes a small space look deeper, like a real landscape.

    6. 6

      Plant from tallest to smallest

      Dig a hole with a spoon or chopstick. Ease each plant out of its pot, gently loosen the roots and shake off extra soil. Set it in the hole and press soil firmly around it. Place taller plants toward the back or center and smaller ones in front, with space between them.

      Why it matters: Loosened roots spread into the new soil. Spacing gives plants room to grow and lets air move, which helps prevent mold.

    7. 7

      Add stones, wood and a top layer

      Place stones or wood to build the scene. Use aquarium-safe or well-cleaned wood, never painted or pressure-treated wood. Cover bare soil with sheet moss or small pebbles.

      Why it matters: Hardscape gives the scene structure. A top layer holds moisture in the soil and keeps it from splashing onto the glass when you water.

    8. 8

      Clean up and water lightly

      Brush soil off the leaves and wipe the inside of the glass. Mist or pour in a small amount of water, just enough to dampen the soil. You shouldn’t see water pooling in the drainage layer.

      Why it matters: It’s easy to add more water and hard to remove it. Too much water is the most common reason terrariums fail.

    9. 9

      Add a cleanup crew (closed jars)

      Springtails and small isopods (pill bugs) can be bought for terrariums. They eat mold and dead leaves. In the builder, the snails, frogs, fireflies and butterflies are just for fun; real frogs need a proper vivarium and expert care, and wild animals should stay outside.

      Why it matters: Decomposers recycle waste into nutrients, which is what makes a closed terrarium close to self-sustaining.

    10. 10

      Pick the right light

      Put it in bright, indirect light, such as near a window but out of direct sun, or under a grow lamp. Succulents in an open container can take more light.

      Why it matters: Plants need light for photosynthesis, but glass traps heat. Direct sun can overheat a closed jar and cook the plants.

    11. 11

      Watch it and keep it balanced

      In a closed jar, some fog in the morning is normal. If the glass stays fogged all day, take the lid off for a few hours. If you never see condensation, add a little water. Trim plants that touch the glass, remove dead leaves and wipe away mold. In an open container, water only when the soil is dry.

      Why it matters: A terrarium is a small ecosystem. Watching the condensation tells you whether the water cycle is in balance.
